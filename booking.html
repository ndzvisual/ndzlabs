NDZ DEVELOPMENT SYSTEM - CASE 01

Appointment Management System

Appointment management tool for independent hairdressers, beauty centers and tattoo studios. MVP in active development with 5 confirmed pilot businesses.

Role

Solo — Ideation, Discovery, Design & Development

Single product lead covering ideation, user discovery, UX design and full-stack development. Coded using AI-assisted development with Opencode. Target market: independent beauty and tattoo businesses in Latin America.

Project Impact

5 Pilot Businesses
50% Conversion Rate
0 Training Required
1 MVP Iteration

The Market

The appointment management software market has a gap most solutions ignore because it's not profitable to serve. Independent hairdressers, beauty centers, tattoo studios and similar businesses need to manage bookings efficiently, but available tools are either too big for their scale or priced unjustifiably.

Large platforms offer payment systems, complex online stores and multi-branch management, but for a solo tattoo artist or a three-chair hairdresser, those aren't features — they're overhead.

The Problem

Small independent service businesses need a way to manage appointments that's quick to set up, simple to use without training and priced according to their scale. Existing solutions have too many features or are too expensive.

Most of these businesses manage bookings through Instagram DMs or WhatsApp. Some use spreadsheets. Almost none use a tool designed for their actual size.

Discovery & Insight

Before writing any code, I reached out to about 10 independent businesses through Instagram and WhatsApp. Five responded positively and agreed to become product pilots.

The conversations confirmed that the main problem wasn't functionality but simplicity — every business had tried at least one existing tool and stopped using it because it was too complex.

The initial concept included analytics, but after talking to pilots I removed it from the MVP scope. None of them mentioned analytics as a need.

MVP Strategy

The MVP's core principle: a business should be able to log in and understand the value immediately, without tutorial, onboarding flow or learning curve.

  • Appointment management for a single location
  • Staff management
  • Customer booking flow
  • Weekly view with appointment details
  • Basic business profile settings

Key Decisions & Trade-offs

01

Simplicity Above All

Adoption vs Capability

The main problem confirmed by all pilots was complexity, not lack of features. An experience with no learning curve was prioritized over breadth of functionality.

02

Single Branch Focus

Scope vs Scale

Multi-branch management isn't relevant for the target segment at this stage. Excluding it reduced complexity significantly without sacrificing value for early users.

03

Analytics Deferred

Validation vs Assumption

The initial concept included analytics. Discovery conversations revealed that no pilot business considered it a need. Deferring it kept the MVP lean and focused.

04

No Onboarding

Trust vs Assistance

No tutorials or onboarding flows. The product must communicate its value through the interface itself. This constraint guided every scope decision.

Implementation

The project is being built vibe-coded end to end using AI-assisted development, following the same approach used in professional projects: structure the problem first, build after.

Project artifacts include PRD, architecture document, UX flows and AGENT.md — a reusable AI agent. The AGENT.md approach came from a lesson learned in a previous INAES project: documenting logic and architecture before code.

Next Steps

The MVP will be delivered to the 5 pilot businesses. The goal isn't to measure conversion or revenue at this stage, but to observe whether businesses can use the product independently from day one, without help, and whether appointment management actually moves from WhatsApp to the tool.

The pilot will inform the first post-launch iteration.

Key Learnings

  • Simplicity is the product, not just a design principle.
  • Discovery conversations that change scope are a sign of learning, not failure.
  • Documenting architecture before code prevents costly errors that emerge during implementation.
  • The right MVP scope is defined by what users confirm they need, not by what the product could do.
